## What the extra $5 and $10 actually buy

Starter to Pro costs $5 more per month and doubles both the core count (4 to 8) and the RAM (4 GB to 8 GB). That is the best value step in the lineup, and it is why Pro carries the "Most Popular" badge on the pricing page.

Pro to Power costs $10 more and takes you to 12 cores and 12 GB. The jump is smaller in relative terms. Eight cores already handle a handful of browser profiles without strain; twelve cores matter when you keep a stack of parallel sessions open, run automation across several accounts, or keep heavier desktop software resident all day.

Storage does not move at all. All three plans come with a 40 GB SSD, and no amount of extra money buys a bigger disk. If your work involves local datasets, video files or long-term archives, this product is the wrong shape for you and the tier table won't fix it.

Two claims on the product page need a caveat. The infrastructure section advertises "up to 16 core Intel E5-269X processors," while the highest configuration actually sold is 12 cores, so treat 12 as the ceiling. The same page says the NVMe storage makes the VPS "5 times faster," which is a marketing line with no published benchmark behind it. Buy on cores, RAM and the IP, which are the parts you can verify.

Take Starter. Twelve monthly payments total $419.40. The annual price is $314.95. The difference is $104.45 for the year, which is exactly the 25% the pricing page advertises.

The same calculation on the other two tiers: Pro costs $479.40 across twelve monthly payments versus $359.95 annually, a $119.45 saving. Power costs $599.40 versus $449.95, a $149.45 saving. None of that gap comes from better hardware. It is purely the billing cycle.

One inconsistency is worth flagging. The pricing page's opening line says you can save 25% by choosing quarterly billing. The three-month prices don't support that: Starter works out to 19% off, Pro and Power to 17% off. The 25% figure only appears in the twelve-month column. Go by the table, where every discount reconciles against the monthly price multiplied by the number of months.

The middle cycles are less dramatic than they look. Moving from three months to six saves $0.83 to $1.66 per month depending on tier. Moving from six months to twelve saves another $1.24 to $2.49. If you're unsure whether the IP will work for your accounts, three months at $84.95 on Starter is a reasonable testing window, and the cost of that flexibility is a few dollars a month.

## Residential IP, the five ISPs, and 20+ locations

The pitch is straightforward: a Windows 10 Pro desktop in the US, connected through a residential ISP rather than a datacenter, so the IP looks like a home connection. The site's own FAQ defines a residential IP as an address tied to a physical device such as a phone or desktop, registered by the ISP in public databases, which lets websites see the provider, network and location. It adds that most online services treat residential addresses as real people, unlike datacenter ranges. That definition is accurate and matches how the industry describes it.

The provider advertises 20+ US residential IP locations and names five states as examples: New York, Florida, Texas, Georgia and Virginia. The ISPs it lists are Spectrum, Century Link, Verizon, AT&T and Comcast Xfinity. Each VPS gets a dedicated IP, meaning it is assigned to you rather than shared, and you get full admin or root access on Windows 10.

What the pages don't publish is the mapping between ISP and state. If your work depends on appearing in a specific city on a specific carrier, such as an AT&T address in Georgia rather than a Verizon address in New York, confirm availability with support before paying, because the public location list doesn't show which combination you'll get.

## Fresh IP: exists, priced privately, and explained badly

There is a separate Fresh IP page marketed at people who want an address never assigned to any other customer, with no survey profile ever created on it. For sensitive accounts, that is a legitimate thing to want.

No price is published for it. No stock count, no list of which ISPs or states offer it. Any article quoting a surcharge figure for Fresh IP is making that number up. Ask support what it costs and whether it's available in your target state.

The FAQ also contradicts itself on what a used IP is. One answer describes a used IP as "a single address used by multiple websites within one web server," with the server parsing requests to the right site. That is a description of shared web hosting, not of a residential IP that a previous customer used. Elsewhere the same page describes a used IP simply as an address previously assigned to a customer that may now have issues on some sites. The second version is the one that applies here.

Practical takeaway: if the account you're logging into is valuable, ask support for the history of the specific IP before it's provisioned, and ask what Fresh IP costs for that location. Get the answer in writing.

## Who should skip this

A residential IP is the entire product. If you just want an anonymous US IP for browsing or streaming, a datacenter VPS costs a fraction of $34.95 and works fine.

Skip it too if you need more than 40 GB of storage, since no tier offers it. Skip it if you need Linux, because the only OS on the menu is Windows 10.
